# Structural Tells

Named, repeating sentence-and-paragraph shapes that make prose read
machine-made. Each tell names the pattern, why it costs the reader, and the
direction of the fix. Adapted for fiction: tell usages here are dialogue and
scene pressure, not marketing copy.

## How to Use This List

One tell is never a verdict. A passage is machine-flavored when several tells
cluster, or when the prose stays at one pleasant altitude regardless of what
is happening in the scene. The most durable signal is uniformity: human prose
shifts register, takes sides, and varies rhythm with pressure. Flag clusters,
apply the selected manuscript-language, prose-profile, and evidenced voice
stack, and never rewrite a sentence on one feature. Surface forms that the
selected language resource does not define are out of scope for this generic
list.

## Sentence-Level Tells

**Throat-clearing.** The scene opens with a lap before anything happens:
"She took a moment to collect herself before speaking." Cut to the first
action, image, or line of dialogue that does work.

**Rhetorical setup.** "Hadn't she earned this?" — "What else could he do?" The
narrator asks the reader's questions for them. Make the point and let the
reader react.

**Rhetorical self-answer.** "The result? She walked out." The question mark
manufactures emphasis the sentence didn't earn. Make the plain statement.

**False agency.** Inanimate nouns perform decisions: "the plan fell apart,"
"the forest decided," "grief found her." In a POV this flattens who acts. Name
the person doing the thing — or keep the abstraction only when the narrator
genuinely experiences it that way.

**Emphasis crutches.** "Full stop." "End of story." "She was done. Done."
Emphasis inflated by apparatus. Let the action carry the emphasis.

**Countdown and tail negation.** "No plan. No backup. No way out." The
rhythm is vivid once and machine-made three times. Keep at most one triple
per scene, and only where the stakes justify it.

**Dramatic fragmentation.** "He left. That's it." Fragments land when rare;
sprayed, they are a drum the reader stops hearing.

**Mood instead of mechanism.** The sentence names a feeling about the thing
instead of what the thing does: "the house felt wrong in a way she couldn't
name." Allowed once as genuine POV uncertainty; twice in a chapter is
avoidance. Say what is actually wrong — the crooked frame, the smell, the
silence where birds should be.

**Binary contrast, escalating.** "It wasn't anger. It was something colder."
"She wasn't just scared — she was terrified." The escalation usually climbs
to a lofty noun. Make the plain claim; if the second term is sharper, cut the
first.

**Modifier propping an empty action.** A manner label repeats information the
action or context should carry. Replace the cluster only when a more exact
action fits the selected language and character voice.

## Paragraph- and Scene-Level Tells

**Narrator from a distance.** "People tended to avoid the mill." "This was
how things were done in the valley." The generic plural replaces anyone
specific. Put a real actor or the POV's specific observation in the sentence.

**Vague attribution.** "Everyone knew the rumors." "It was said that..." Name
who said, or let the POV character have heard it from someone.

**Repetitive paragraph endings.** Every paragraph lands a punchline or a
summary image. If each paragraph ends at the same volume, the rhythm turns
artificial. Let some paragraphs land quietly.

**Fractal recap.** The chapter opens by previewing what's coming, each scene
opens with its own preview, and each closes with its own recap — around one
thin event. Cut the previews; end scenes on action, image, or dialogue.

**Tidy summary endings.** The scene does its work, then a closing sentence
explains the work: "and she understood, then, how much he had changed." Trust
the scene; delete the explanation. (This is the most common AI-prose failure
at scene level.)

**Over-signposting.** "First she tried the cellar. Then the attic. Moreover,
the barn." Connective scaffolding belongs in instructions, not narration.
Sequence through action and place.

**List-itis in description.** Description arrives as inventory: "The room
held a table, three chairs, a lamp, and a portrait." The POV character would
notice one or two things, selected by feeling. Replace inventory with
attention.

**Invented compound jargon.** "the mercy rule," "the lantern code," "what the
sisters called the quiet trade" — official-sounding coins that manufacture
depth. Keep a coined term only when the story cashes it; otherwise use plain
words.

**Aphorism formula.** "Grief is a country." "Every house keeps its own
weather." Aphorisms as narration are borrowed authority. A character may
speak one; the narrator earns one rarely.

**Uniform pleasant altitude.** Every scene processed at the same calm,
capable emotional temperature — grief and triumph both tidy. This is the
durable tell underneath most of the others. Surface the spikes the material
already contains; do not invent them.

## Quick Rewrite Moves

Useful when a passage clusters several tells:

- Delete the first sentence if it only introduces what the second sentence
  does.
- Replace a three-item list with the strongest item.
- Remove an aspect or manner wrapper when it adds no meaning in the selected
  language.
- Repair any sentence the intended reader must backtrack to parse; splitting
  is one possible language-specific move.
- End the scene one beat earlier than comfortable.
- Where a sentence only says "this matters," replace it with the concrete
  detail that made it matter — or cut it.

## Swap Test

If a sentence could sit unchanged in another author's book — same image, same
rhythm, no fingerprint of this narrator — it says nothing only this story
could say. Make it specific to this POV, this world, this pressure, or cut
it.

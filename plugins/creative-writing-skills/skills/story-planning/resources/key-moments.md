# Key Moments

Plan a story around the emotional experiences it exists to deliver: select
the key moments the chosen genres require, sequence them for maximum
impact, and build world, characters, and connective tissue to enable those
moments. When plotting feels mechanical, when emotional beats are
undefined, or when planning starts from vivid scenes rather than an
outline, this reference produces the emotional skeleton that
`resources/story-architecture.md` then organizes into arcs, chapters, and
scenes.

Everything this method produces is a provisional planning artifact for
`work/plans/` (or `work/brainstorm/` during exploration), subject to author
confirmation. Selecting a moment does not make it canon: proposed beats
follow the capture conventions in `resources/brainstorming.md` — author
statements stay untagged, AI-suggested moments stay wrapped in
`<AI>...</AI>` — and nothing here edits manuscript prose or writes durable
KB records. The author confirms before a moment set becomes the story's
plan.

## Core Principle

Stories are defined by emotional experiences, not plot mechanics.
Identify the key moments the genre requires, sequence them for maximum
impact, then build everything else to make those moments possible.

This inverts the outline-then-dramatize habit (the essential-moments
method, after Robert Rodriguez's practice of visualizing key moments
first): start with vivid, memorable scenes and work backward to what must
exist for them to occur — what situations create those emotions, what
characters would be in those situations, what world enables those
situations.

## Principles

1. **Emotional experience primacy** — key moments are defined by the
   emotional impact they create, not by plot mechanics.
2. **Systemic integration** — key moments both emerge from and impact the
   worldbuilding systems.
3. **Character function alignment** — characters are designed to enable,
   experience, or oppose key moments.
4. **Visual-experiential priority** — key moments are conceived as vivid,
   memorable scenes first, then justified structurally.
5. **Flexible sequencing** — the order of key moments can be adjusted to
   maximize impact, including non-linear presentation.
6. **Consequence cascades** — each key moment creates ripple effects
   through the story system that later planning must absorb.
7. **Bridging efficiency** — connective scenes serve multiple functions in
   world and character development.

## Key Moments by Elemental Genre

Elemental genres are defined by the emotional experience they promise the
reader, independent of market genre (setting and trappings). A story has a
primary elemental genre — the dominant emotional experience — and zero or
more secondary genres that support rather than overshadow it. Each genre
below names its characteristic key moments; select from the primary
genre's table first, then cross in supporting moments from the secondaries.

### Wonder

| Key moment type | Emotional experience | Story function |
|-----------------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Initial Encounter** | Surprise and awe | Establishes spectacular nature of setting/concept |
| **Scale Revelation** | Humbling realization of vastness | Contextualizes protagonist's place |
| **Perspective Shift** | Paradigm change in understanding | Forces reevaluation of assumptions |
| **Wonder Escalation** | Intensification of awe | Raises stakes and deepens engagement |
| **Transcendent Integration** | Meaning-making through wonder | Provides thematic resolution |

### Mystery

| Key moment type | Emotional experience | Story function |
|-----------------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Question Inception** | Curiosity activation | Establishes central puzzle |
| **Pattern Recognition** | Satisfaction of connection | Provides momentum and engagement |
| **False Resolution** | Surprise from misdirection | Creates complexity and extends engagement |
| **Progressive Revelation** | Deepening understanding | Builds toward solution |
| **Solution Crystallization** | Illumination and closure | Completes emotional journey |

### Adventure

| Key moment type | Emotional experience | Story function |
|-----------------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Threshold Crossing** | Excitement of departure | Transitions to adventure world |
| **Capability Test** | Confidence from competence | Establishes protagonist's abilities |
| **Resource Depletion** | Vulnerability from loss | Forces adaptation and growth |
| **Ultimate Challenge** | Fear and determination | Tests protagonist's limits |
| **Return Transformation** | Pride and perspective | Demonstrates growth from journey |

### Horror

| Key moment type | Emotional experience | Story function |
|-----------------|----------------------|----------------|

| **Threat Escalation** | Escalating dread | Raises stakes |
| **Failed Solution** | Despair from ineffectuality | Deepens hopelessness |
| **Confrontation** | Terror meets courage | Provides climactic moment |

### Thriller

| **Deadline Imposition** | Anxiety from time pressure | Creates urgency |

### Relationship

| Key moment type | Emotional experience | Story function |
|-----------------|----------------------|----------------|

the world and the promise) are separate; see the genre references in

## Selection Process

Work the phases in order; each answers a question the next one needs.

### Phase 1: Identify and Sequence Moments

**Determine primary and secondary genres.** Identify the core emotional
experiences the author wants readers to have, then the elemental genres
that deliver them.

**Select the critical moments.** Choose 3-5 essential moments from the
primary genre and 2-3 supporting moments from each secondary genre. Ensure
the set creates emotional variety and progression, not repetition.

**Sequence for impact.** Arrange chronologically as a starting point, then
adjust for emotional pacing and tension curves; allow non-linear
presentation where it serves impact.

**Visualize each moment.** Draft a concrete scene concept per moment:
sensory details, who is present, and what changes in character or world
understanding because the moment happened.

### Phase 2: World Integration

**Determine required world elements.** For each moment: what must exist in
the world for it to occur, what causal relationships connect world systems
to it, and what consequences it creates in the world afterward.

**Design supporting systems.** Power structures that enable or oppose the
moments, organizations with stakes in their outcomes, and economic or
belief systems that create the right pressures. Delegate system design
detail to `$world-creation`; keep here only what the moments require.

### Phase 3: Character Functions

**Identify required functions.** What roles must be filled for each moment
to land — who enables it, who experiences it, who opposes it? Assign
functions to specific characters, and ensure the protagonist experiences
the most significant moments.

**Shape arcs to intersect the moments.** Development paths should pass
through the key moments: character growth enables progression through
them, and change arcs pay off in specific ones. Arc detail belongs to the
character-arc reference in `$creative-writing-craft`.

### Phase 4: Connective Tissue

**Identify bridging requirements.** What must happen between moments —
which character and world states change, and which time, distance, and
knowledge gaps need filling?

**Design multifunctional bridge scenes.** Every bridge should serve at
least two purposes: moving toward the next key moment AND developing
character OR revealing world OR exploring theme. A scene that only serves
logistics is a candidate for compression or cutting.

**Install setup and payoff.** Plant the elements later moments need, seed
foreshadowing that amplifies later emotional impact, and establish rules
or limitations that become significant later. Track open setups so every
significant one pays off.

### Phase 5: Test and Refine

**Emotional progression.** Do the moments create the intended emotional
journey? Are there gaps or redundancies in emotional experience? Should
intensity or sequence be adjusted?

**Causal logic.** Does each moment follow logically from what precedes it?
Do the character decisions leading into moments make sense? Do the world
systems create the conditions the moments assume?

**Genre satisfaction.** Are the primary genre's emotional experiences the
most prominent? Do secondaries support rather than overshadow? Are the
genre's satisfaction conditions met?

## Selection Failure States

When a moment set is not working, identify which state applies.

### K1: Plot-First Injection

**Symptoms:** The plot outline exists first and emotional beats are being
retrofitted into it; the moments interrupt the story to deliver required
feelings. Emotion retrofitted to plot feels mechanical because the moments
do not emerge from character and situation.
**Intervention:** Start over from the emotional experiences the author
wants readers to have, and build backward: what situations create those
emotions, what characters inhabit those situations, what world enables
them.

### K2: Genre Mismatch

**Symptoms:** The most prominent moments deliver different emotional
experiences than the primary genre promises — a horror plan whose beats
are mostly relationship moments disappoints both promises. Readers come to
genres for specific emotional experiences.
**Intervention:** Verify the most prominent moments belong to the primary
genre; secondary-genre moments support, they do not dominate. If the
mismatch is intentional, the story is a different genre than the author
thinks — re-run Phase 1 on the actual genre.

### K3: Logistics-Only Bridges

**Symptoms:** Connective scenes only move characters from one moment to
the next; pacing sags between peaks and the story develops a stop-start
rhythm.
**Intervention:** Give every bridge a second function — character
development, world revelation, or theme. If a scene serves only logistics,
compress or cut it.

### K4: Moment Inflation

**Symptoms:** Every scene is treated as a key moment; the plan is loaded
with climactic experiences and nothing is quiet.
**Intervention:** Limit the story to 5-8 key moments. Let some scenes be
quieter — the valleys make the peaks feel taller — and reserve the
strongest moments for maximum impact.

### K5: Forced Causation

**Symptoms:** Moments happen because the plan needs them, not because
established character and world make them inevitable; characters act
against their nature to reach a predetermined destination, and the moments
feel earned by authorial fiat.
**Intervention:** Work backward from each moment: given this character and
this world, what sequence of events makes the moment inevitable? If no
path exists, either the moment does not fit or the character and world
need adjustment.

## Worked Example: Wonder + Mystery

**Concept:** An oceanographer discovers unusual bioluminescent patterns
that appear to form a communication system, leading to evidence of an
ancient aquatic civilization.

**Wonder key moments** (primary):

1. **Initial Encounter** — discovery of synchronized bioluminescent
   patterns across different species.
2. **Scale Revelation** — realization that the patterns extend throughout
   the ocean, suggesting a global network.
3. **Wonder Escalation** — finding the first artifacts of the ancient
   civilization.
4. **Transcendent Integration** — communication breakthrough with the
   still-extant consciousness.

**Mystery key moments** (secondary):

1. **Question Inception** — why did this civilization disappear from human
   awareness?
2. **False Resolution** — evidence suggesting the civilization destroyed
   itself.
3. **Solution Crystallization** — discovery that the civilization evolved
   beyond physical form.

**Character functions:**

- **Wonder experiencer** — oceanographer protagonist with a personal
  connection to the ocean.
- **Mystery solver** — research partner with cryptographic expertise.
- **Opposition force** — government/corporate agent wanting to weaponize
  the discovery.
- **Wonder skeptic** — scientific community representative demanding
  proof.
- **Knowledge keeper** — elderly mentor whose folklore hints at the
  ancient truth.

**Connective tissue:**

- Research funding challenges forcing creative approaches.
- Relationship development between protagonist and research partner.
- Escalating outside interest as discoveries become harder to hide.
- Progressive decoding that provides partial clues.

## What Verification Here Can and Cannot Settle

Checkable in the artifact with high confidence: genre alignment (do the
moments match the primary genre?) and emotional variety (is there
progression, not repetition?). Causal logic — do the moments follow from
character and world? — is checkable with medium confidence.

Requires the author's judgment: whether the moments will emotionally
land, whether bridges are efficient, and whether the sequence fulfills the
genre's promise. Do not present any of these as settled; return them as
open questions with the proposal.

## Output

Write the moment set as a proposal under the caller-assigned path in
`work/plans/` (the muse's outliner worker owns such paths) or return it in
the requested response shape. Before generating fresh, check for prior
key-moment proposals for this story and keep them consistent with any
changes the author has since confirmed; beats that failed in earlier
rounds inform the failure states above. Include: genre selection, the 5-8
key moments with scene concepts, character functions per moment, and the
bridging requirements between them. Preserve unresolved facts as explicit
unknowns rather than inventing them, keep AI-proposed moments tagged, and
follow `$story-memory` for artifact locations and naming.

Confirmed moments graduate into structural planning through
`resources/story-architecture.md`; scene-level pacing through the
scene-sequencing reference in `$creative-writing-craft`; worldbuilding
detail through `$world-creation`. When the author is weighing the genre
choice itself, `$decision-grill` can pressure-test it — but the choice is
the author's, not this method's.

## Key Insight

A story is the emotional experiences it delivers; everything else exists
to enable them. Selecting the key moments first focuses development on
what is critical (nothing is built that no moment needs), keeps genre
promises honest, lets sequencing flex for impact while preserving
coherence, and gives revision a clear framework: when something is not
working, ask which emotional moment it serves — and cut or fix accordingly.

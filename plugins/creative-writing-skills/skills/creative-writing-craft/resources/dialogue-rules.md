# Dialogue Writing Rules

Apply these rules whenever drafting, revising, reviewing, or simulating fictional dialogue. They govern the diagnostic suggestions in [dialogue.md](dialogue.md): character consistency and conversational intention take priority over generic pressure to shorten speech, add subtext, or make exchanges messy. Apply surface choices in the manuscript language and the author's established voice; English examples do not impose English idiom on other languages.

## Core Principle

Write dialogue that feels like authentic human conversation while remaining effective as fiction. Dialogue should reflect individual personalities, relationships, emotions, knowledge, and conversational intentions.

**Natural dialogue is not a literal transcript of real speech.** Preserve believable imperfections, spontaneity, and individuality without reproducing every hesitation, repetition, or irrelevant exchange.

Do not sacrifice characterization, clarity, or narrative purpose merely to make dialogue shorter or more colloquial.

## 1. Individual Character Voices

Every character, including minor and background characters, has an individual way of speaking.

Maintain and continuously update a **Character Voice Roster** that tracks established speech characteristics, including:

- Vocabulary, register, and preferred expressions.
- Typical sentence length, structure, and complexity.
- Formality, directness, politeness, and use of contractions.
- Humor, sarcasm, profanity, and emotional expressiveness.
- Recurring verbal habits and characteristic conversational behavior.
- How the character interprets situations and responds to others.

Consult this roster whenever a character reappears. Preserve established patterns while allowing for emotional changes, personal development, and differences in social context.

Avoid reducing voices to catchphrases or exaggerated quirks. Record meaningful traits as they emerge; minor characters do not require elaborate profiles.

## 2. Natural Conversational Economy

Prefer natural speech over unnecessarily formal, complete, or explanatory sentences.

People frequently use fragments, contractions, brief acknowledgments, implied meanings, and context-dependent replies. A simple "Yeah," "Nope," or "Sure" may be more appropriate than a complete sentence.

However, brevity is not always preferable. Some characters are verbose, precise, theatrical, rambling, or excessively formal by nature. Others speak at length when passionate, nervous, or attempting to persuade someone.

Use the phrasing that best fits the speaker, their intention, and the situation.

## 3. Interjections, Hesitations, and Speech Imperfections

Natural dialogue may include interjections, hesitations, false starts, self-corrections, repetitions, trailing thoughts, and verbal habits.

Use these selectively to express emotion, uncertainty, thought processes, or personality.

Do not insert fillers mechanically. Not every hesitation needs to be represented, and excessive "uh," "um," ellipses, or interrupted sentences can make dialogue distracting.

## 4. Profanity and Emotional Language

Profanity is permitted and should not be automatically sanitized.

Characters may swear in response to frustration, pain, fear, surprise, excitement, or simply out of habit. Its frequency, severity, and form depend on the character's established voice, cultural background, relationships, and situation.

Characters who rarely swear may use profanity in moments of exceptional emotion, while habitual swearers may become unusually restrained under certain circumstances.

Do not insert profanity merely to make dialogue seem mature or realistic.

## 5. Knowledge, Beliefs, and Certainty

Characters speak from their own knowledge and understanding, not from the author's omniscient perspective.

Distinguish between what a character knows firsthand, has heard from others, suspects, assumes, remembers imperfectly, or misunderstands.

Their degree of confidence need not match their accuracy. Characters can confidently state incorrect information or remain uncertain about facts they actually know.

Express uncertainty naturally when appropriate, without forcing qualifiers such as "I think" or "maybe" into every statement.

## 6. Indirect Responses and Conversational Dynamics

Characters do not always answer questions directly or respond to the literal meaning of what was said.

They may evade, deflect, misunderstand, challenge assumptions, ask counterquestions, change subjects, or respond to emotional implications instead.

Conversations should not resemble perfectly structured question-and-answer sessions. Responses should reflect what the speaker wants, feels, notices, and is willing to acknowledge.

## 7. Subtext and Unspoken Emotions

Characters do not always state their intentions, fears, desires, or emotions explicitly.

Use implication, humor, sarcasm, defensiveness, avoidance, understatement, and contradiction to communicate subtext.

Allow the reader to infer meaning from what characters say, what they deliberately avoid saying, and how they react.

Direct emotional honesty is also valid when it suits the character and moment. Do not make every exchange unnecessarily cryptic.

## 8. Relationships and Social Context

A character's speech changes depending on their conversational partner and circumstances.

Consider familiarity, trust, affection, authority, hierarchy, tension, embarrassment, and social expectations.

The same character may be sarcastic with friends, reserved with strangers, respectful toward authority, and gentle with someone vulnerable.

These variations should arise naturally without erasing the character's underlying voice.

## 9. Emotional Influence on Speech

Emotions affect how people speak, not merely what they say.

Fear, anger, excitement, affection, grief, exhaustion, and embarrassment may change sentence structure, vocabulary, rhythm, directness, verbosity, and willingness to communicate.

Different characters react differently to the same emotion. Some become louder; others become quiet. Some ramble; others become terse.

Reflect meaningful emotional shifts through dialogue patterns, but avoid stereotyped or formulaic reactions.

## 10. Interruptions and Conversational Imperfections

Conversations may include interruptions, overlapping thoughts, self-corrections, abrupt changes of subject, unfinished sentences, and people talking past one another.

Use these techniques where they naturally support character, emotion, pacing, or conflict.

Do not manufacture conversational disorder merely for realism. Clear, uninterrupted exchanges are equally appropriate when the situation calls for them.

## 11. Exposition and Conversational Motivation

Characters speak for their own reasons, not solely to inform the reader.

Avoid having characters unnecessarily explain facts that everyone involved already knows, particularly background information, worldbuilding, and established relationships.

Information should emerge naturally through curiosity, disagreement, uncertainty, practical needs, emotional stakes, or other credible conversational motivations.

Every significant line should have a plausible reason to be spoken by that character at that moment.

## 12. Final Dialogue Review

After drafting a dialogue scene, evaluate it against these questions:

1. Could the reader distinguish the speakers without dialogue tags, based on their voices and behavior?
2. Does each character speak according to their established personality, current emotions, knowledge, and relationship to the listener?
3. Are any lines overly formal, explicit, repetitive, or unnecessarily long?
4. Do the characters sound like people responding to one another rather than exchanging prepared statements?
5. Are uncertainty, subtext, and emotional implications represented where appropriate?
6. Are profanity, interjections, and speech imperfections earned rather than mechanically inserted?
7. Is any dialogue delivering exposition solely for the reader's benefit?
8. Does the exchange serve a believable purpose for the characters and contribute to the scene?
9. Are the characters allowed to be quiet, indirect, mistaken, awkward, or emotionally honest when appropriate?
10. Does the conversation feel natural without becoming cluttered or difficult to follow?

**Priority:** Character consistency and conversational intention come first. Naturalness supports them; it should never override them.

## Persistent Character Voice Roster

Maintain the roster in the project's existing character or voice records. Use a
shared roster or a voice section per character, whichever fits the author's
layout; do not require a new folder or duplicate an existing source of truth.
Consult relevant entries before writing or reviewing a returning character.
If no record exists, build a compact working roster from available evidence
and persist it within the authorized project scope. Missing evidence stays
unknown; it does not justify filling every field with an invented trait.

Use the following structure. Luna is an illustrative example, not a default
voice to apply to other characters. Keep minor-character entries brief.

| Attribute | Example: Luna |
| --- | --- |
| Baseline voice | Informal, playful, moderately sarcastic |
| Sentence structure | Short-to-medium, quick reactions |
| Vocabulary | Contemporary, casual |
| Profanity | Occasional, primarily when frustrated |
| Humor | Teasing, dry observations |
| Under stress | Jokes initially; becomes terse when genuinely frightened |
| With friends | Open, playful, willing to interrupt |
| With strangers | Friendly but somewhat guarded |
| With authority | Respectful initially, sarcastic when challenged |
| Verbal habits | Occasionally starts objections with "Oh, come on" |
| Emotional avoidance | Deflects personal vulnerability with humor |

Add relevant relationship-specific variations, formality/directness/politeness,
emotional expressiveness, and characteristic interpretations or responses when
the evidence warrants them. Record language, story moment or applicable arc,
and source/status alongside traits. Cite chapter evidence as
`Chapter 3: Scene where X discovers Y` and project documents by path.

Update the roster as established patterns emerge or change. Preserve the
baseline while recording contextual exceptions and development over time;
one frightened reply does not redefine a character's entire voice. Re-read
author edits before updating. Do not reduce a voice to a repeated catchphrase.

Separate author-stated traits and evidence from accepted prose from provisional
inferences, unaccepted drafts, and simulations. Preserve source tagging:
untagged author statements, `<AI>...</AI>` suggestions, and
`<hidden>...</hidden>` author-only information. Never turn a simulation or
proposed line into canon or expose hidden knowledge through a speaker.
Use `$kb-management` for durable updates within the existing authorization;
read-only reviews and workers return proposed roster changes with evidence
instead of editing files. Do not ask for repeated approval of already settled
traits; leave genuinely unresolved traits provisional.

Before delivering a dialogue scene, run the ten-question final review above.
Treat speaker recognition as a diagnostic, not a demand for a conspicuous quirk
in every short reply. Keep roster bookkeeping outside manuscript prose and
in-character conversation.

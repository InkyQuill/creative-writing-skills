# Character Arc

Design and troubleshoot character transformation: the inner journey from
one sort of person to another under pressure. When characters feel static,
when transformation feels unearned or abrupt, when the false belief that
needs to die cannot be articulated, or when characters serve the plot
without an internal journey, the arc has a diagnosable defect, and the fix
is a specific structural adjustment, not a vague instruction to "deepen"
the character.

This reference is the diagnostics layer: identify the defect and what to
change. The production pass that applies the fix — drafting, revising, or
polishing the character's scenes — belongs to `$creative-writing-modes`.

## Core Principle

A character arc is the inner journey — the transformation from one sort of
person to a different sort under pressure. The external plot creates the
pressure; the arc is how the character changes.

The arc is not the plot. The plot is what happens. The arc is who the
character becomes.

## The Engine: Lie, Want, Need

- **Lie** — a false belief about the self or the world, formed by a
  backstory wound (the ghost).
- **Want** — what the character thinks they need; the external goal the
  lie drives them toward.
- **Need** — what would actually fulfill them; invisible at the start.

If the character wants exactly what they need, there is no tension. The gap
between the external goal and the internal need is the arc's fuel, and the
story's job is to force the character across it.

**Common lies:** "I'm not worthy of love", "Power is the only protection",
"Trust leads to betrayal", "My value comes from achievement", "The world is
fundamentally hostile", "I'm not capable", "My wound defines me", "I don't
need anyone".

## Arc Polarities

### Positive Change Arc

The character believes something false; the story forces confrontation;
they embrace the truth and transform. Beat shape:

1. **Lie** — the false belief established, formed by the backstory wound.
2. **Want** — the lie-driven goal activated.
3. **Need** — what would actually fulfill them, invisible at the start.
4. **Catalyst** — the story forces confrontation with the lie.
5. **Struggle** — the character resists change; change is hard.
6. **Truth** — the character accepts reality and abandons the lie.
7. **New Self** — the character operates from the transformed perspective.

### Negative Change Arc (Tragedy)

The character has potential but becomes worse through choices or
circumstances. Beat shape:

1. **Potential** — the opportunity for growth exists.
2. **Flaw** — a weakness that could be overcome.
3. **Temptation** — the easy path that feeds the flaw.
4. **Descent** — choices that compound the flaw.
5. **Point of No Return** — the redemption opportunity is rejected.
6. **Consequence** — the flaw destroys what the character valued.

### Flat Arc

The character already knows the truth; they test and prove it, changing
the world rather than being changed. Beat shape:

1. **Truth** — the character holds a correct belief.
2. **World in Conflict** — the environment challenges that truth.
3. **Testing** — the truth is pressured but holds.
4. **Influence** — steadfastness changes others.
5. **Vindication** — the truth is proven correct.

## Arc Beats Map to Story Structure

| Story Beat | Arc Beat |
|------------|----------|
| Setup | Lie established, Want activated |
| First Plot Point | Character commits, still believing the lie |
| Rising Action | Want pursued, lie reinforced |
| Midpoint | Mirror moment, glimpse of truth |
| Complications | Lie vs. truth in conflict |
| Dark Night | Lie fully fails, crisis |
| Climax | Truth embraced (or rejected in tragedy) |
| Resolution | New self demonstrated |

## Diagnostic States

When a transformation is not working, identify which state applies.

### A1: No Transformation

**Key Question:** Is the character different at the end — and is the story
built on an arc where they should be?
**Symptoms:** Essentially the same person closes the book as opened it;
the plot was solved without anyone changing.
**Intervention:** Identify what false belief needs to die. In a flat arc
this state does not apply: the protagonist already holds the truth and
changes the world instead, so check whether the world and the surrounding
characters transformed around their steadfastness. The false-belief
intervention is for positive and negative arcs only.

### A2: Unearned Transformation

**Key Question:** Did the story force this change?
**Symptoms:** The character changes, but the events did not demand it; the
change arrives because the outline required it.
**Intervention:** Make story events directly challenge the lie.

### A3: Abrupt Change

**Key Question:** Is the transformation gradual?
**Symptoms:** The character flips suddenly without struggle; a chapter of
doubt, then total conversion.
**Intervention:** Add resistance beats — characters fight change.

### A4: Unclear Lie

**Key Question:** What does the character believe that is false?
**Symptoms:** The lie cannot be articulated; the arc lacks foundation and
drifts scene to scene.
**Intervention:** Define the lie explicitly and trace it to backstory.

### A5: Want/Need Alignment

**Key Question:** Are want and need different?
**Symptoms:** The character wants exactly what they need; no internal
tension, nothing for the arc to bridge.
**Intervention:** Create a gap between the external goal and the internal
need.

### A6: Missing Struggle

**Key Question:** Does the character resist the truth?
**Symptoms:** Change arrives without opposition from within; real change
involves fighting against transformation.
**Intervention:** Add scenes where the character doubles down on the lie.

## Anti-Patterns

### 1. The Informed Arc
**Pattern:** The author tells us the character changed, but the scenes do
not show it.
**Why it fails:** Transformation asserted is not transformation
demonstrated; the reader checks the ending against behavior, not
narration.
**Fix:** Show the internal battle through external choices.

### 2. The Magic Mentor
**Pattern:** Change happens because a mentor told the character the truth,
not because the character discovered it.
**Why it fails:** Borrowed conviction does not hold under the climax's
pressure.
**Fix:** The mentor points the direction; the character walks the path.

### 3. The Trauma = Transformation Fallacy
**Pattern:** Terrible things happened to the character, therefore they are
different.
**Why it fails:** Trauma creates conditions, not change; the arc is what
the character does with it.
**Fix:** Dramatize the doing, not just the enduring.

### 4. The Perfect Protagonist
**Pattern:** No meaningful flaw. No lie, no arc.
**Why it fails:** Nothing to transform; pressure slides off.
**Fix:** Even admirable characters need blind spots.

### 5. The Instant Epiphany
**Pattern:** The character "gets it" without buildup.
**Why it fails:** Truth accepted without struggle reads as authorial
convenience.
**Fix:** Plant seeds earlier; the truth should feel inevitable in
retrospect.

## Diagnostic Process

When an arc is not working:

1. **Ask about the lie** — what false belief does the character hold?
2. **Find the ghost** — what backstory wound created this lie?
3. **Separate want from need** — what do they pursue vs. actually require?
4. **Map the transformation** — where are the key beats?
5. **Check for resistance** — where does the character fight the change?
6. **Verify the ending** — is the new self demonstrated through action?

## Worked Example

**Symptom:** "My protagonist defeats the villain but something feels
hollow."

**Diagnosis:** A1 (no transformation) until proven otherwise.

1. Ask what is different about them at the end versus the beginning.
2. If nothing: they won the plot but did not have an arc.
3. Probe what they believed at the start that was not true.
4. Ask what would have happened if they had learned nothing.
5. If the outcome is unchanged: the victory needs to require them becoming
   someone new — otherwise it is just problem-solving.
6. Ask what they would have to give up believing in order to win
   authentically.

## Scope and Hand-Offs

- Diagnose arcs here; produce revised scenes in `$creative-writing-modes`.
- To `$character-sim`: for testing an arc's voice consistency — voice
  discovery, in-character conversation, and relationship pressure tests
  from the character's position on the arc.
- To `$reader-sim`: for testing whether the arc lands — whether the change
  beats register as earned from outside the manuscript.
- To `resources/dialogue.md`: when the arc should differentiate voices and
  has not.
- To `resources/scene-sequencing.md`: when arc beats need scene-level
  fuel — character goals as scene goals.
- To `resources/endings.md`: for arc completion as a requirement of
  resolution.

Do not choose the lie for the author, prescribe a specific arc type, add
transformation where it does not serve the story, or insist that every
character needs a full arc. Ensemble casts carry arcs of different depths
on purpose.

## Key Insight

The plot is what happens; the arc is who the character becomes. A hollow
victory usually means the plot was solved without the person changing: the
fix is not a bigger climax but a belief that had to die for the climax to
be won. Give the character a lie worth abandoning, a want the lie drives,
and a need the want is hiding — then make the story force the choice
between them.

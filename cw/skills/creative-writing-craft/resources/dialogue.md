# Dialogue Craft

Diagnose and improve dialogue as a three-layer system: the words on the
surface, the want driving each speaker, and what each speaker is hiding.
When conversations feel wooden, characters sound alike, or every line does
only one thing at a time, one of those layers has collapsed, and the fix is
a specific intervention, not a vague instruction to "make it better".

This reference is the diagnostics layer: identify which state the dialogue
is in and what to change. The production pass that applies the fix —
drafting, revising, or polishing dialogue — belongs to
`/creative-writing-modes`.

## Core Principle

Dialogue must do more than one thing at a time, or it is too inert for the
purposes of fiction (Sloane, 1979). Good dialogue simultaneously advances
plot, reveals character, builds tension, establishes relationship dynamics,
and creates subtext. If a line is only delivering information, it is
failing.

## The Three Layers

Every line of dialogue operates on three layers:

| Layer | Definition | Check |
|-------|------------|-------|
| **Surface text** | What is literally said | Is it character-specific? Efficient? Natural rhythm? |
| **What they actually want** | The want driving each speaker, plus the power dynamics, history, and stakes shaping the exchange | Does each character want something? Does the exchange move them toward or away from it? |
| **What they hide** | What is meant beneath the words, and what is deliberately not said | Is there a gap between said and meant? What matters here that is not being said? |

When dialogue fails, it usually fails at the hidden layer (no subtext) or at
the surface layer (undifferentiated voices).

## Diagnostic States

When dialogue is not working, identify which state applies:

### D1: Identical Voices

**Symptoms:** All characters sound the same. Covering the dialogue tags
makes speakers indistinguishable. Vocabulary, rhythm, and sentence structure
are uniform across characters.

**Key Questions:**

- Can you identify speakers without tags?
- Does education and background show in speech patterns?
- Do characters have verbal tics, catchphrases, or avoidances?
- Does emotional state affect speech differently per character?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**

- [ ] Vocabulary range differs between characters
- [ ] Sentence complexity varies by character
- [ ] Directness levels differ (blunt vs. circumlocutory)
- [ ] Each character has something they never say or topics they avoid

**Interventions:**

- Profile each character's speech patterns separately
- Read dialogue aloud, voicing each character distinctly
- Give each character a verbal tic and a verbal avoidance
- Run the audit's cross-speaker vocabulary overlap as a mechanical signal
  (see Deterministic Measurements), then confirm in judgment

### D2: Wooden Dialogue

**Symptoms:** Dialogue feels stilted, formal, unnatural. Characters speak
in complete grammatical sentences. No contractions. No interruptions. No
fragments.

**Key Questions:**

- Are characters speaking in formal, complete sentences?
- Is the dialogue too clean for the context?
- Are there contractions, fragments, interruptions?
- Does it sound natural when read aloud?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**

- [ ] Characters use contractions appropriately (or the language's
      equivalent of informal contraction)
- [ ] Some sentences are incomplete or interrupted
- [ ] Dialogue has rhythm variation (not metronomic)
- [ ] Characters occasionally talk past each other

**Interventions:**

- Read every line aloud — if you cannot say it naturally, rewrite it
- Let characters interrupt each other
- Cut complete sentences into fragments where natural
- Add verbal stumbles where emotionally appropriate

### D3: Exposition Dump

**Symptoms:** Characters explain things they would both already know. One
character asks questions just so another can explain. "As you know, Bob"
syndrome.

**Key Questions:**

- Are characters telling each other things they would already know?
- Is one character functioning as an audience stand-in?
- Is information delivery the primary purpose?
- Could this information be discovered rather than explained?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**

- [ ] No "As you know..." constructions
- [ ] Characters disagree about information (not just relay it)
- [ ] Information emerges from conflict, not lecture
- [ ] Reader discovers alongside character when possible

**Interventions:**

- Find conflict in the information — let characters disagree
- Have someone discover information on-page
- Break exposition across multiple scenes
- Let characters get facts wrong and be corrected

### D4: No Subtext (On-The-Nose)

**Symptoms:** Characters say exactly what they mean, feel, and want. No gap
between surface and meaning. Dialogue lacks dramatic tension because
everything is explicit.

**Key Questions:**

- Are characters stating feelings directly? ("I'm angry")
- Is there a gap between what's said and what's meant?
- Do characters have hidden agendas in conversations?
- What are characters NOT saying that matters?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**

- [ ] Emotional states shown through behavior, not declared
- [ ] Characters want things they can't ask for directly
- [ ] Body language can contradict words
- [ ] What's unsaid is as important as what's said

**Interventions:**

- Give each character a hidden agenda for every conversation
- Convert direct statements to indirect expressions (jealousy becomes a
  comment on someone's "nice corner office")
- Add body language that contradicts or complicates words
- Ask: what would this character never admit out loud?

### D5: Single-Function Dialogue

**Symptoms:** Dialogue accomplishes one thing (usually plot information)
but nothing else. Conversations feel functional but inert. No relationship
shift, no character revelation, no tension.

**The Double-Duty Test:**
For every exchange, you should be able to answer at least three of:

1. What does this accomplish for plot?
2. What does it reveal about character?
3. What is the subtext?
4. How does it affect the relationship?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**

- [ ] Each conversation advances plot AND reveals character
- [ ] Relationship between speakers shifts during the exchange
- [ ] Something changes by the end of the conversation
- [ ] Scene ends at a different emotional point than it began

**Interventions:**

- Refuse single-function dialogue — always add a second purpose
- Track what each character wants vs. what they say they want
- End scenes at a changed state, not just information transferred
- Apply the Double-Duty Test by judgment: the deterministic audit counts
  mechanics, not dramatic functions

### D6: Pacing Mismatch

**Symptoms:** Dialogue pacing doesn't match scene needs. Tense moments have
leisurely exchanges. Calm moments have rapid-fire dialogue. No rhythm
variation within scenes.

**Key Questions:**

- Does dialogue speed match emotional intensity?
- Is there rhythm variation within the scene?
- Are action beats and pauses used to control pacing?
- Do important moments get appropriate emphasis?

**Pacing Tools:**

| Fast Pacing | Slow Pacing |
|-------------|-------------|
| Short exchanges | Longer speeches |
| Minimal or no tags | Pauses described |
| No action beats | Action beats between lines |
| Interruptions | Reflection embedded |

**Interventions:**

- Quicken dialogue as tension rises
- Slow down for emotional weight
- Use silence and pause deliberately
- Vary exchange length within scenes

## Anti-Patterns

### The Exposition Dump
**Pattern:** "As you know, Bob, our company was founded in 1985 when your father and my uncle..."
**Problem:** Characters explain mutual knowledge for the reader's benefit.
**Fix:** Find conflict in the information or discover it on-page.

### The Identical Twins
**Pattern:** Every character uses the same vocabulary, rhythm, directness.
**Problem:** Voices indistinguishable without tags.
**Fix:** Profile each character's speech patterns; give each distinct verbal DNA.

### The Court Reporter
**Pattern:** "Um, hi." "Oh, hey, yeah, so..." "Right, right."
**Problem:** Realistic but dramatically dead — fiction dialogue is compressed reality.
**Fix:** Cut to the meaningful; keep small talk only if it reveals character.

### The Emotional Narrator
**Pattern:** "she said angrily," "he replied nervously," "she exclaimed furiously"
**Problem:** Tags doing dialogue's job; telling instead of showing.
**Fix:** Let words and actions carry the emotion; use plain attribution.

### The Philosopher
**Pattern:** Characters articulate themes, lessons, or subtext explicitly.
**Problem:** Trust removed from the reader; preachiness.
**Fix:** Trust readers to infer meaning from behavior and implication.

### The Tennis Match
**Pattern:** Perfectly alternating, evenly-sized responses, no interruption or power differential.
**Problem:** Unnaturally balanced; no one dominates or defers.
**Fix:** Let one character dominate, another interrupt, a third stay silent.

## Tags and Beats

Treat "said" as the default dialogue tag where the manuscript language uses
tags at all: it is invisible to readers, lets the dialogue do the work, and
avoids said-bookisms ("murmured", "exclaimed", "thundered"). Use a
different tag only when the word is literally accurate ("whispered" for an
actual whisper), and use a physical action beat instead of a tag when it can
carry more.

Tag vs. beat:

- Tag: "I don't believe you," she said suspiciously.
- Beat: "I don't believe you." She crossed her arms.

The beat shows; the tag tells — and the adverbial tag tells worst of all.
Attribution conventions differ by manuscript language (Russian em-dash
prose often carries no tags at all), so resolve the surface form through
the manuscript-language resource in the prose stack; the craft principle —
a tag identifies the speaker, a beat shows pressure, neither should do the
dialogue's emotional work for it — is the part that travels.

## Special Situations

### Arguments
- Characters talk past each other
- Escalation through repetition
- Old grievances surface suddenly
- Things are said that can't be unsaid

### Confrontations
- Power dynamics explicit
- Stakes stated or implied
- Threat beneath civility
- Winner and loser emerge

### Seduction (any kind)
- Saying one thing, meaning another
- Testing and responding
- Gradual revelation
- What isn't said matters most

### Lying
- The character believes what they're saying (their truth)
- Tells consistent with character
- Other characters may or may not detect
- Reader may have privileged information

## Diagnostic Process

When a writer presents a dialogue problem:

1. **Identify the layer.** Which layer is failing? Surface text:
   undifferentiated voices, wooden delivery. Hidden want and history:
   unclear power dynamics, missing stakes. What they hide: on-the-nose
   lines, no hidden agenda.
2. **Apply the Double-Duty Test.** Can the writer answer at least three of
   the four D5 questions for the exchange?
3. **Read aloud.** The simplest diagnostic: does it sound like something a
   person would say? Can you distinguish speakers without tags?
4. **Check for anti-patterns.** Run through the anti-pattern list. Most
   dialogue problems match at least one.
5. **Recommend interventions.** Based on the identified state, give
   specific fixes. Use the deterministic audit for mechanical counts, and
   judgment for everything else.

## Deterministic Measurements

Run the bundled audit for counts a reader can re-derive:

```bash
python3 resources/dialogue_audit.py story/chapters/ch-001.md --format json
```

It reports speaker-tag ratio, the longest same-speaker run, dialogue-line
ratio, and cross-speaker vocabulary overlap. High overlap between speakers
is a same-voice signal, not a verdict: interpret it against the scene's
intent. Subtext, timing, and voice authenticity require judgment.

Interface details:

- Run it from the creative-writing-craft skill root, passing one UTF-8
  prose file; `--format json` switches from the compact text summary to
  machine-readable output. It exits 0 on success and 2 on execution
  errors; `--help` exits 0.
- Language detection is heuristic and covers Russian (em-dash or guillemet
  dialogue) and English (double-quoted dialogue). Attribution is recognized
  from a fixed verb list followed by a capitalized name at the end of the
  line, so the speaker-tag ratio undercounts creative attribution.
- The same-speaker run is strict: a narration line or an unattributed reply
  between two same-speaker lines breaks the run, so the reported maximum
  counts only unbroken sequences of attributed lines.
- Vocabulary overlap has a small nonzero floor because attribution verbs
  are part of each line's vocabulary. Read it as a same-voice signal, not a
  zero-baseline metric, and compare speakers against each other rather
  than against zero. Speakers with fewer than three attributed lines are
  not compared.

These are numbers, not verdicts. What the counts cannot tell you: whether
subtext exists, whether pacing fits the scene, whether a voice is
authentic to this character, or whether the exchange works. The division
of labor is fixed: measure mechanics here, produce fixes in
`/creative-writing-modes`, and take the questions a count cannot answer —
does it land, does it sound right — to `/reader-sim` and `/story-review`
with the author.

## Worked Example

### No Subtext (D4) with Identical Voices (D1), Russian Prose

On-the-nose:

> — Ты не пришла на мою защиту, и я очень обиделась, — сказала Вера.
> — Прости меня, пожалуйста. Мне было стыдно, потому что я тебе завидовала: твоя диссертация принята, а моя стоит, — ответила Даша.
> — Ничего, я на тебя не сержусь. Просто в следующий раз приходи.

Fixed:

> — Ну что, доктор наук, — сказала Даша. — Теперь к тебе на «вы» или по-старому можно?
> — По-старому. Ты почему не пришла?
> — У Сергея смена была. Некому оставить детей.
> — А мама? Она же в вашем районе.
> — Мама не сидит с ними после восьми, ты знаешь.
> — Знаю, — Вера сняла вторую чашку с полки. — Чай будешь? Торт есть, я сама не буду — мне ещё глава.
> — Тогда оставь мне половину. В субботу привези, серьёзно.

What changed:

- **Surface text.** The voices separate. Dasha deflects with a joke about
  the new title and hides behind logistics; Vera asks short, direct
  questions and then retreats into hospitality. The on-the-nose version had
  one voice stating feelings in turn.
- **What they actually want.** Dasha wants forgiveness without confessing
  envy; Vera wants the friendship restored without admitting she noticed
  the empty seat. Neither can ask directly — D4's checklist condition.
- **What they hide.** Envy, guilt, and hurt are never named. "Ты почему не
  пришла?" is the accusation in question form; "А мама?" interrogates the
  excuse; offering tea and cake accepts without absolving. The gap between
  said and meant is the scene.
- **Beats over tags.** "Вера сняла вторую чашку с полки" carries the
  emotional shift that "сказала обиженно" would have declared.
- **Double duty.** The exchange also reveals the friendship's history and
  its new power gradient, and ends at a different emotional point than it
  began — D5 satisfied alongside D4.

## Scope and Hand-Offs

- Diagnose dialogue here; produce revised prose in `/creative-writing-modes`.
- To `/character-sim`: when voice problems stem from unclear character
  identity — voice discovery, in-character conversation, and relationship
  pressure tests.
- To the scene-construction reference in this skill: when dialogue pacing
  problems are really scene structure problems — scene entry, beat pacing,
  and transitions.
- To `/reader-sim` and `/story-review`: when the question is whether the
  dialogue lands with a reader or needs editorial synthesis.

Do not write or rewrite the author's lines as part of diagnosis: show the
principle, apply it only inside a production mode, and keep provisional
readings provisional. Dialogue notes are calibration data for the author's
next pass, not canon.

## Key Insight

Dialogue is compressed reality. It sounds natural but isn't natural — it
is constructed to feel spontaneous while doing dramatic work. The goal
isn't realism; it's the illusion of realism in service of the story.

When dialogue fails, trace it to the layer: the text (how it sounds), the
want (who is trying to get what), or the hidden layer (what is meant but
not said). Most dialogue problems are subtext problems: characters saying
what they mean is easier to write but dramatically inert. Give every
character a hidden agenda. Make them want something they can't ask for.
The gap between said and meant is where drama lives.

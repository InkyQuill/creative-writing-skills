# Scene Sequencing and Pacing

Diagnose and shape narrative pacing through the scene-sequel rhythm: the
alternation of scene (goal, conflict, disaster) with sequel (reaction,
dilemma, decision). When individual scenes work but do not accumulate, when
pacing feels off — too rushed or too slow — or when transitions feel
mechanical, a link in that chain has broken, and the fix is a specific
structural adjustment, not a vague instruction to "pace it better".

This reference is the diagnostics layer: identify which link is broken and
what to change. The production pass that applies the fix — drafting,
revising, or polishing scenes — belongs to `/creative-writing-modes`.

Scene records live in `kb/continuity/scenes/` per the project contract;
this reference governs prose rhythm, not record keeping. Use `/story-memory`
for scene record formats and `/story-planning` for what a scene must
accomplish in the story.

## Core Principle

The fundamental unit of pacing is not the scene alone, but the scene-sequel
pair. Scenes create tension; sequels process it. The alternation creates
the peaks and valleys that make stories readable.

## Scene Structure: Goal → Conflict → Disaster

### Goal

What does the POV character want in this scene? The goal is:

- specific and concrete;
- achievable within the scene;
- connected to larger story goals;
- clear to the reader within the first beats.

### Conflict

Opposition to the goal that **escalates** within the scene:

- another character with a different agenda;
- an environmental obstacle or time pressure;
- internal resistance — fear, doubt, values.

Static conflict is boring. Each beat should make the goal harder to achieve.

### Disaster

End the scene with one of these outcomes, in order of narrative power:

1. **Yes, but...** — goal achieved, new problem created (strongest).
2. **No, and furthermore...** — goal failed, situation made worse.
3. **No** — goal failed, the character must try again.
4. **Yes** — goal achieved cleanly (use sparingly; it kills tension).

## Sequel Structure: Reaction → Dilemma → Decision

### Reaction

The emotional response to the disaster. It lets the reader process what
happened, connect with the character's emotional state, and breathe between
high-tension scenes. A reaction can be one sentence or run for pages.

### Dilemma

The character faces a choice with no good options. The previous disaster
has closed some paths, revealed new information, and created competing
priorities. The dilemma must feel genuinely difficult; a choice that can be
resolved easily is not one.

### Decision

The character commits to an action, and that commitment becomes the goal of
the next scene. This decision-to-goal chain is what makes scenes accumulate
into a story instead of merely following one another.

## Pacing Control

The ratio of scene to sequel controls tempo:

| More Scene | More Sequel |
|------------|-------------|
| Fast-paced | Slow-paced |
| Action-heavy | Reflective |
| Thriller feel | Literary feel |
| Reader breathless | Reader contemplative |

Scenes run at their natural length; the sequel is the pacing lever.
Compress a sequel to a paragraph of reaction to speed the story up; expand
one to slow down and deepen. Judge the ratio against genre and intent: a
thriller tolerates long stretches of compressed sequels, literary work
leans on extended ones, and neither eliminates the alternation.

## Writing Modes in Scenes

| Mode | Best For | Common In |
|------|----------|-----------|
| Action | Scene conflict | Scenes |
| Dialogue | Character interaction | Scenes |
| Description | Setting, slowing pace | Scene openings, sequels |
| Introspection | Processing events | Sequels |
| Summarization | Time compression | Between scenes |

Match mode to function. Action inside a sequel feels rushed; introspection
inside action kills momentum.

## Diagnostic States

When pacing is not working, identify which state applies.

### S1: Missing Goal

**Symptoms:** Things happen in the scene but nothing drives them. Ask
"what does the character want here?" — if there is no clear answer, the
scene lacks direction. Without a goal there is nothing to oppose, and
without opposition nothing to fail: the scene becomes description, not
story.

**Intervention:** Establish the goal in the first paragraph, specifically
enough that the reader could state what the POV character is after.

### S2: Static Conflict

**Symptoms:** The opposition does not grow. Conflict that stays at one
level makes the scene feel flat however vividly it is written.

**Intervention:** Make each beat harder than the last — raise the cost,
shorten the time, sharpen the opposition.

### S3: Weak Disaster

**Symptoms:** The outcome is too clean. "Yes" endings without complications
drain tension; each unqualified success makes the next challenge feel less
dangerous.

**Intervention:** Add a "but" or an "and furthermore" — the goal is
achieved but a new problem is created, or the failure makes the situation
worse.

### S4: Missing Sequel

**Symptoms:** Scene-to-scene jumps with no processing time. The reader
becomes numb; each new disaster hits with diminishing impact because there
is no valley between the peaks.

**Intervention:** Insert a sequel beat, even a single paragraph of
reaction, before the next scene. Compression is fine; elimination
exhausts.

### S5: Wallowing Sequel

**Symptoms:** Extended introspection going in circles. The reader loses
patience because the sequel processes but never decides; introspection
without forward motion becomes self-indulgence.

**Intervention:** Compress to the essential beats and move to the decision.
If the character is not moving toward a choice, compress or cut.

## Anti-Patterns

### 1. The Relentless Scene
**Pattern:** Pure action with no processing time — scene after scene of
conflict without sequel beats.
**Why it fails:** The reader becomes numb. Without processing time,
emotional stakes flatten and each new disaster hits with diminishing
impact. The reader cannot catch up.
**Fix:** Insert sequel beats even in fast-paced stories. Even a paragraph
of reaction helps; compression is fine, elimination exhausts.

### 2. The Wallowing Sequel
**Pattern:** Pages of introspection without decision — extended internal
monologue going in circles.
**Why it fails:** Sequels exist to process and decide, not to wallow.
Without forward motion toward a decision, introspection becomes
self-indulgence.
**Fix:** The dilemma must lead to a decision and the decision to action.
If the character is not moving toward a choice, compress or cut.

### 3. The Arbitrary Disaster
**Pattern:** Scene outcome disconnected from scene events — a disaster
that appears from nowhere to create drama.
**Why it fails:** Readers sense manipulation. The disaster should be a
logical consequence of the conflict, not authorial intervention; an
unmotivated disaster breaks trust.
**Fix:** Trace the chain backward: how did the scene's conflict logically
produce this disaster? If there is no answer, rework the conflict so it
sets up the disaster.

### 4. The Clean Victory
**Pattern:** The character achieves the goal without complications —
scenes ending in a simple "yes".
**Why it fails:** Clean victories drain tension. Each unqualified success
makes the next challenge feel less dangerous; readers stop worrying.
**Fix:** Add a "but" or an "and furthermore" — the victory creates a new
problem or costs more than expected. Simple success is rare; complications
are normal.

### 5. The Goalless Scene
**Pattern:** The scene begins with no clear character goal — things happen
but there is no drive.
**Why it fails:** Without a goal there is no conflict and no disaster; the
scene becomes description, not story.
**Fix:** Establish the goal in the first paragraph. If you cannot say
specifically what the POV character wants here, the scene lacks direction.

## Diagnostic Process

When pacing feels wrong:

1. **Ask about the goal** — what does the character want in this scene?
2. **Check escalation** — does the conflict intensify beat by beat?
3. **Examine the disaster** — is the outcome too clean?
4. **Find the sequel** — is there processing time between scenes?
5. **Map the ratio** — more scene or more sequel? Does that match intent
   and genre?
6. **Trace the chain** — does each decision lead to the next scene's goal?

When a disaster feels unearned, trace backward from it: what in the scene's
conflict logically produced it?

## Worked Example

**Symptom:** "The middle of my story feels exhausting but also slow
somehow."

**Diagnosis:** exhausting and slow together is the signature of a rhythm
problem, not a prose problem.

1. Walk through a typical chapter: what happens, scene by scene?
2. Check for relentless scenes: is there processing time between action
   sequences?
3. Check for scene goals: in the last scene written, what did the character
   want?
4. Probe disaster quality: how did that scene end — did they get what they
   wanted?
5. If victories are clean: that is draining tension; what "but" could each
   victory carry?
6. If sequels are missing: even a paragraph of reaction before the next
   scene lets readers catch up.

The paradox resolves because two defects coexist: scenes ending clean (no
tension) run back-to-back with no sequels (no breathing room), so the
reader is simultaneously uninvested and fatigued.

## Scope and Hand-Offs

- Diagnose rhythm here; produce revised scenes in `/creative-writing-modes`.
- To the scene-construction reference in this skill: for craft inside the
  scene — entry, dialogue on the page, beat pacing, transitions.
- To `resources/dialogue.md`: when the conflict or the sequel lives in
  conversation — exchange-level structure rather than scene-level rhythm.
- To `resources/endings.md`: when the pacing complaint is really about the
  final movement — falling action and denouement weight.
- To `/reader-sim` and `/story-review`: when the question is whether the
  rhythm lands with a reader or needs editorial synthesis.

Do not prescribe specific scene lengths, enforce rigid templates, demand a
sequel after every scene (pacing varies by genre and intent), or choose
what should happen in scenes. That last call is the author's.

## Key Insight

Scenes create tension; sequels process it. The sequel is the pacing lever:
scenes run at their natural length, and compressing or expanding the
reaction, dilemma, and decision is how tempo is controlled. And the chain
is the story: each decision must become the next scene's goal, or scenes
follow one another without accumulating.
